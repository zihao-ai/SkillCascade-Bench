PERSONAL STATEMENT:
The summer I turned sixteen, I sat in a molecular biology laboratory at a university three states from home — an experience that reframed my understanding of what science actually looks like in practice, as opposed to what I had encountered in textbook diagrams. The distinction, I discovered, is considerable.

In the laboratory, hypotheses were not conclusions waiting to be confirmed; they were instruments of inquiry, provisional and subject to rigorous revision. Our team spent two weeks attempting to isolate a protein complex whose behavior confounded the published literature, and the dissonance between expectation and data was, paradoxically, the most intellectually generative aspect of the entire experience.

That summer crystallized for me a disposition I had long suspected but never articulated: I am most alive intellectually when I am uncertain — when a question resists the first three answers I give it and demands a fourth. I bring that orientation to everything I pursue, from my coursework in multivariable calculus to my reading in philosophy of science.

I am drawn to this program because of its emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration. I am ready not merely to receive an education but to contribute to one.
